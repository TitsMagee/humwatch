What the research says

Studies of long-term low-frequency noise are few, and they don't settle what it does to health. Most look at annoyance. These are good places to start. None of them studied this hum, and the readings on this page can't show any effect on anyone's health.

  • Health effects from low-frequency noise and infrasound in the general population: is it time to listen?

    Baliatsas and others, 2016, Science of the Total Environment. A systematic review of seven studies. It found links with annoyance, sleep problems, poor concentration and headaches in adults living near low-frequency sources. The authors call the evidence scarce and limited, especially for long-term illness.

  • A review of published research on low frequency noise and its effects

    Leventhall, Pelmear and Benton, 2003. A review of the research, written for the UK government's environment department.

  • Proposed criteria for the assessment of low frequency noise disturbance

    Moorhouse, Waddington and Adams, University of Salford, 2005. Written for the same UK department, and still cited as guidance for assessing low-frequency noise.

  • Environmental noise guidelines for the European Region

    World Health Organization, 2018. These cover traffic, wind turbine and leisure noise, not industrial hum, but they are the main reference on long-term noise and health.

How these readings work

A microphone outside listens all day and night. Every minute, a small computer works out which of the hum's known tones are present, and how far they stand above a reference taken while the source was stopped.

  • These figures compare readings from the same microphone. They are not a certified noise measurement, and they are not dB(A).
  • Passing planes, traffic and other short sounds are largely filtered out, because they don't produce the same steady set of tones.
  • A change takes several minutes to show here, and start and stop times are approximate.
  • When the readings can't be trusted, this page says so rather than guessing.
